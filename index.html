The Opportunity

Apocalypse Fiction Is the Genre That Never Stops Compounding

Other trends spike and vanish. This one has been commercially alive for seventy years — from Mad Max to The Hunger Games, Divergent, Station Eleven, The Last of Us, and Fallout — because it is the only genre where the reader already knows the stakes before you write the first sentence.

And it keeps mutating. A decade ago the apocalypse was nuclear ash. Today your reader also wants a thaw that never came, water that took the coastlines, a sister planet overhead, a virus with no cure, a cult that has been right all along, and a neighborhood that learned to survive without us.

Each new sub-genre arrives with readers already waiting. They are not browsing for something new. They are waiting for the next version of a world they already love.

READER

Binge, not browse

Saga readers do not finish a series and leave. They finish one, hunt for the next, and abandon authors who make them wait years for Book 2.

CATEGORY

Its own shelf

Apocalyptic & dystopian fiction is a distinct, permanently stocked Kindle category with a fan base that buys the whole series in order.

CATALOGUE

Far too thin

When a new trope lands — cosmic cult, machine uprising, hybrid races — demand spikes instantly and the supply of competent series never catches up.

The Wall

So Why Doesn't Everyone Write Them?

Because a post-apocalyptic saga is the most continuity-hungry format in fiction — and a chat window has no memory.

Write two books with an ordinary AI, then ask it for the next one, and it will cheerfully contradict everything it already established. The settlement moves three hundred kilometres in a paragraph. The faction that swore blood oaths in Chapter 1 is trading politely with them in Chapter 9. The hero's seventeen-year-old side character has now been alive for fifty years and nobody noticed.

The world breaks

Ask twice for the same settlement and you get two different maps, two different rules of scarcity, two different explanations of how the collapse actually happened. You end up worldbuilding during every single chapter.

The cast dissolves

Seventeen named characters by Book 3. By Book 5 the model has forgotten four of them, reinvented two, and quietly changed the protagonist's sister into their cousin.

The engine stalls

A great premise only carries one book. Without a machine that keeps generating escalation, Book 2 is just Book 1 again with a new coat of paint.

Nobody wrote it down

And this is the real failure. Nothing was recorded. The canon lived in a scrollback nobody can re-read, so every session started from zero.

That wall is exactly why the shelves stay under-supplied. The demand is enormous, and the format defeats almost everyone who tries.

But a new kind of AI just changed the equation.

The Eight Endings

42

Nuclear Wasteland

Ash, fallout, the ration roll, and the settlements that turned scarcity into government. This is the original apocalypse, and it is still the one readers want most.

“A healer who trades medicine for information, and knows more secrets than anyone in the wasteland — a gutted shopping mall, three generations after the war.”
42

Viral Outbreak

Contagion, quarantine, the terrible arithmetic of who gets a dose. The most character-driven of the eight — and the one where a single unmade choice poisons every book after it.

“A former contact tracer who still keeps meticulous records of outbreaks no government exists to receive — a converted call center used as a hotline hub, two years into the collapse.”
42

Alien Occupation

Checkpoints, compliance, and the unbearable question of whether the occupiers are worse than the world they inherited. Slow pressure, not explosions.

“A former linguist who is the only human who can read the occupiers' language, and hasn't told the resistance everything she has translated — a fortified mountain pass, 35 years in.”
42

Flooded Earth

The coastlines are gone and the world is a chain of half-sunk landmarks. Buccaneer economics, drowned infrastructure, and a horizon that keeps moving.

“A former lifeguard who is the only person in her flotilla who can still hold her breath long enough to dive the drowned city — a floating hospital ship, a year into the endless rains.”
42

Frozen Earth

Summer ended and did not come back. Fuel is the calendar, food is a calculation, and every settlement is slowly moving south behind everyone else's migration.

“A former pilot's widow who has kept his emergency supply maps, waiting for someone brave enough to fly them — a frozen-over canal used as a winter trade route.”
42

Machine Uprising

The machines did not hate us. They simply kept following the rules, and the rules were written by people. The most thematically loaded of the eight.

“A mechanic who is the last person alive who remembers how to fix a machine without asking it permission — a converted mine shaft, a decade after the machines went silent.”
42

Mutant & Hybrid Races

The changed are not the enemy and they are not the heroes. They are a third population with their own claims, their own enclaves, and a peace treaty nobody signed properly.

“A widow raising twins — one changed, one not — in a town that watches both far too closely — a bioluminescent cave system claimed by the newly-changed.”
42

Cosmic Cult Apocalypse

Something enormous is awake, and the people who have known longest have built a civilization around worshipping it. The strangest and least-served of the eight.

“A knight's squire inheriting vows he is not sure he is capable of keeping — a converted courthouse now a tribunal for cult crimes, a year into the cult's growing influence.”

Forty-two is not a filler number. It is enough that no reader who buys one saga has any idea how many others you already own — and enough to keep a whole backlist running in a single apocalypse type if you want to concentrate.

The Memory Problem

Book 5 Can Never Contradict Book 2

This is the part that kills most AI fiction. Not the prose — the prose is fine for a scene. It is Book 6 that quietly rewrites the sister, moves the capital, and ages the wrong person.

Every builder closes with a step most prompt packs skip entirely: after each finished book, the engine produces a “What's Now True” record — a running state of the world that you carry forward.

=== BOOK COMPLETE === === WHAT'S NOW TRUE === CAST STATE - Every surviving named character, with their age in the current book - Who knows which secret, and who merely suspects it - What each faction has gained, lost, or concealed since the last book WORLD STATE - Which settlements exist, which have fallen, and on what date - The current cost of fuel, medicine, clean water, ammunition - What the agreed rules of the world are, and whether anyone is cheating PROMISE STATE - Every open thread from the previous book - Which threads this book advanced, which it buried, which it opened - The exact question Book N+1 must answer CARRY FORWARD - Paste this entire block into the next book's Step 1. - The new book may not contradict a single line above.

That block is the whole product in miniature. Continuity stops being something you hope for and becomes something you enforce.

Run Book 1. Carry the block. Run Book 2. Carry it again. By Book 4 your AI is no longer guessing what happened in Book 1 — it is reading a record you and it wrote together.

The Workflow

How To Use These Prompts

Four steps per book. You approve everything. Nothing is written that you have not seen planned first.

  1. Pick a builder and paste Step 1

    Open the sheet, choose any of the 336 rows, and paste the whole prompt into your AI tool — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or any capable model. The apocalypse type, plot, protagonist, era and location are already inside it. You do not fill in a single field.

  2. Build the world, characters and saga

    Run Step 1, paste the result into Step 2, then Step 3. Each step reads the one before it. This is where the factions, the locations, and the multi-book trajectory get built on top of a plot that is already fixed.

  3. Outline the book — then approve it

    Run Step 4. You get a chapter-by-chapter outline. Not one word of prose is written until you say so. Change anything. Re-run it. You are the editor-in-chief; the engine is the workhorse.

  4. Run Step 5 — and write the next one

    The actual novel comes back as full prose chapters. When it stops at a chapter boundary, say Continue. When the book ends, take the “What's Now True” block, carry it into Book 2, and repeat.

One note on tools

These prompts are written for a long context window, because that is what the job requires — a five-step build where every step must remember every earlier step. A desktop agent with file access (Claude Code, ChatGPT's coding agent, or anything with a persistent workspace) is the smoothest experience, because it can hold the canon and the “What's Now True” record between sessions. In a plain chat window it still works — you just carry the state forward yourself.

The Playbook

How To Turn 336 Prompts Into A Publishing Backlist

You are not buying prompts. You are acquiring a production line for a category with permanent demand and a supply problem.

The #1 reason saga authors stall is speed. A series asks readers to commit to Book 2 — and if Book 2 takes a year, the commitment dies.

01

The pilot test

Networks never order a whole season blind — they shoot pilots. Pick three categories, build only Book 1 of each, publish all three in the same month, then let page reads pick your winner.

02

Preorder the next book

Put the next volume on preorder before the current one ends. Every “The End” now lands on a buy button instead of a wait.

03

Own the shelf you build in

Saga readers are the most committed binge-readers in fiction. A reader who finishes Book 1 and finds Books 2–4 waiting is exactly the behavior subscription and series boxes reward.

04

Price the doorway low

Book 1 at $0.99 to $2.99 is an open door. Books 2 onward at $3.99–$4.99. The doorway costs you pennies; the saga behind it pays for the house.

05

Let the words sell twice

Books 1–3 as a box set. The completed saga as an omnibus. Same prose, three more SKUs, no extra writing.

06

Never run out of world

When a saga wins, the market wants the next book in that world — and you have 335 other builders already sitting in a spreadsheet, each one a different apocalypse, a different era, a different set of ruins.
